How to decide what to eat every day

Why daily food decisions wear you out, and the rules, rotations and tools that make them almost automatic.

By the What2Eat team · Published 17 November 2026 · 7 min read

Deciding what to eat once is easy. Deciding two or three times a day, every day, for a whole family, is not. If you find yourself standing at the fridge with no idea what to make, the problem usually isn't a lack of ideas. It's too many open choices.

Why deciding what to eat is tiring

Every meal decision balances several things at once: what you feel like, what everyone else will eat, how much time you have, what's in the kitchen, cost, health and what you ate yesterday. Repeating that balancing act all day leads to what psychologists often call decision fatigue. When you're tired of deciding, you default to the easiest option, which is often takeaway or the same three dishes on repeat.

The solution is to make fewer decisions, not better ones.

Use rules to remove choices

Simple rules cut most options before you even start thinking.

  • Day rules: "Monday is daal, Friday is something special."
  • Protein rotation: lentils, chicken, vegetables, beef or mutton, eggs, fish. Don't repeat the same one two days running.
  • Time rules: on workdays, dinner must take 30 minutes or less.
  • Leftover rule: one meal a week is made from what's already cooked.

Keep two short lists

  1. The favourites list: ten to fifteen dishes everyone in your household enjoys. When you can't decide, pick from here.
  2. The never list: dishes someone won't eat. Writing them down stops you suggesting them again.

Update both lists every month. Add new hits; drop dishes everyone is bored of.

Hand the decision to something else

Sometimes the easiest way to decide is to let something decide for you. Some families take turns choosing. Others use a meal plan made at the weekend. And some use a meal decider app that picks a dish based on simple settings.

A daily routine that works

  1. Morning: decide dinner. Check if anything needs defrosting or soaking.
  2. Lunch: eat leftovers or a simple rotation dish.
  3. Evening: cook the dinner you chose this morning. No new decisions needed.

When family members disagree

Give everyone a turn. Each person picks one dinner a week from the favourites list. On the other nights, the cook decides. Agreeing on the rules in advance avoids the nightly debate.

Quick answers

How do I stop overthinking what to eat?

Limit your options with rules and a short favourites list, decide earlier in the day, and stick with the decision.
